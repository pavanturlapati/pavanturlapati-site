*A parent's view on AI and children, and a call to action for families, teachers, schools, AI companies, governments and society.*

> **In short**
>
> - AI can be an excellent tutor, but it can also quietly replace learning.
> - A polished submission measures the output, not necessarily understanding.
> - My proposal, for any AI provider: every under-18 user should be linked to a parent or guardian, and guest mode and shared adult accounts should assess intent and escalate to a parent when needed.
> - AI should act as a mentor to the child, with the parent's agreement, and keep the parent in the loop on key moments, much like a sleepover invitation.
> - Companies, parents, teachers, schools, governments and society each have a distinct job.
> - The test: after using AI, is the child more capable of the *next* task alone?

No account. No sign-up. Just a text box.

That is how a person can start using one of the most powerful technologies ever built. Several AI assistants now offer some form of guest or logged-out access, usually with a more limited experience. Anyone can open such a tool, including a teenager, or a child in primary school.

As a parent, that stopped me. A child is still learning why effort, honesty and understanding matter, yet now has a tool that can explain, write, solve and build almost anything in seconds. The answer arrives polished and convincing.

So here is the question that worries me: **did the child learn, or did the child simply obtain the result?**

This is not an argument against AI. It can be an extraordinary tutor: explaining ideas in different ways, letting students practise and offering help beyond school hours. I want my own child to have that.

But the same tool that can support learning can quietly replace it. And a child cannot be expected to know, automatically, how to use something this powerful wisely.

## Nobody has the time to check

Schools and colleges warn students about AI use. But who really has the time to look at *how* a problem was solved, or to check whether the child truly understood the concept?

Teachers have dozens of students. Parents have jobs. A warning on a syllabus cannot audit every homework session. When a finished answer is one prompt away, the warning loses to convenience.

Imagine a student asked to explain photosynthesis. AI produces a polished answer, the student submits it and gets a good grade. If the student cannot explain it the next day, the assignment measured the output, not the understanding.

Students have always been able to copy or memorise. AI changes the scale, speed and sophistication of that shortcut. Banning AI, or asking students to promise they did not use it, is not enough. We need systems that make understanding visible.

## What is in place today, and the gap

The industry is starting to respond. Many AI providers state that their services are not intended for young children, require parental consent for teenagers, or offer a teen experience with age-appropriate safeguards and optional parental controls. These policies vary from one provider to another, and product features change quickly.

These are meaningful steps. But my concern is not about any one company. It is about the pattern across the industry: **parent linking is typically optional, and guest access sits outside it.** Protection depends on a family discovering and configuring it. Guest access alone does not prove safeguards are bypassed, but a child can often reach powerful AI without a parent ever being involved. That should not depend on a family's awareness or technical skill, and it applies to every AI provider.

There is a second, quieter gap: **the adult account.** A child can simply use a parent's phone, laptop or logged-in AI account, often without the parent knowing. The AI then assumes it is talking to an adult, with no teen safeguards, no age-appropriate behaviour and no parent in the loop. A parent who never set up a child's account may not even realise their child is using AI at all.

## My proposal for AI companies

These apply to every company building AI that children can reach. They are my recommendations, not statements of any company's existing policy.

**1. Parent-linked accounts for every under-18, by default.** Where a user is a minor, the account should be linked to a parent or guardian. This should be the standard, not an optional setting. Age assurance is imperfect, so companies should explain its limits and offer an easy way to correct mistakes.

**2. Don't just trust the account: assess who is actually using it.** This matters in guest mode and on adult accounts. If the conversation suggests a likely minor or sustained schoolwork, such as homework-style questions, a child's phrasing or topics typical of school, the AI should pause and ask for a parent's approval or authentication before continuing, and offer to link the account to a parent. It should treat these as signals, not proof, and give an easy way for a genuine adult to confirm and carry on. Casual, low-risk questions need not trigger this. The goal is proportionate escalation, not blocking everyone.

**3. Give parents a summary, not surveillance.** Once linked, a parent should receive a clear **learning summary**: what topics the child worked on, how they used AI, and any safety concern. Parents should not need to read every message. Summaries and proportionate safety alerts respect a child's growing privacy while keeping a parent genuinely informed.

**4. Be a mentor, not an answer machine.** With a parent's agreement, the AI should act like a good mentor to the child. When a child asks for homework help, it should ask what they have tried, offer a hint, explain the next step, ask the child to try it and check understanding. It can still give direct answers when appropriate, but copying should not be the easiest path in every educational context.

**5. Keep the parent in the loop on key moments, like a sleepover invitation.** When a child is invited to a party or a sleepover, we expect the other family to ask us first. AI should work the same way. Routine help should flow freely, but for significant moments the AI should seek the parent's acknowledgment and agreement first: moving into sensitive topics, changing how it works with the child (for example a new learning plan or a long-term project), or any request that goes beyond schoolwork. The parent agrees once to the mentoring relationship and then stays informed, rather than being asked about every message.

**6. Be transparent and publish evidence.** Children should understand that the assistant is AI, that it can be wrong, and that confident language is not proof. Companies should publish whether their safeguards work and whether students learn more, and allow independent researchers to assess this while protecting privacy.

## What parents should do

Parents cannot become full-time monitors. But we can build habits that last beyond any product:

- **Talk openly about AI.** Ask what they use it for and when they feel tempted to copy.
- **Ask them to teach it back.** "Can you explain this in your own words?" is more revealing than checking the homework looks right.
- **Agree on boundaries.** Decide when AI helps, and when independent effort comes first.
- **Explore the tools together.** Show how to ask for hints, check sources and spot uncertainty.
- **Model responsible use.** Show that AI output needs judgement and verification.
- **Secure your own accounts and devices.** If your child can open your logged-in AI app, the AI will treat them as you. Use separate profiles, screen locks and, where available, a child profile linked to your account.

The aim is enough trust that a child can say, "I didn't understand this, so I asked AI to do it", and we can work through it together.

## What teachers and schools should change

If schools assess mainly the final document, they will struggle to tell learning from AI-generated work. Teachers need not reject AI, but they can make the process matter as much as the product: oral explanations, a related problem solved without AI, notes on which AI suggestions were accepted or rejected, or correcting an error in an AI response.

Schools should teach **AI literacy** explicitly: how AI is useful, why it can be wrong, how to verify claims, how to protect personal information and how to disclose AI help. Policies should state when AI is allowed and how to acknowledge it. Teachers need training and time, not just instructions to detect AI use.

## What government and society should do

This cannot be left to individual families. Access to devices, adult time and digital skills varies widely, and a model that assumes every parent can supervise every interaction leaves many children behind.

I am not asking for one country's rules. I am asking that **local laws support human growth**: clear, enforceable, age-appropriate expectations for services used by minors, covering privacy, transparent age assurance, proportionate safeguards and independent evaluation. They should be developed with educators, child-development experts, families and young people, and they should fund AI literacy for parents and teachers.

Society has a role too. We should stop treating a polished assignment as proof of ability. Employers, universities and families should value the process of thinking, not just a finished result. And we should avoid moral panic: young people need guidance and practice, not only restrictions.

## A shared responsibility

- **AI companies:** linked accounts for minors, intent-aware accounts, a mentoring role agreed with parents, parent summaries and key-moment approvals, published evidence.
- **Parents:** boundaries, curiosity and conversations.
- **Teachers:** AI literacy and checking understanding.
- **Schools:** clear policies, training and process-based assessment.
- **Governments:** proportionate safeguards, privacy protection and accountability.
- **Society:** valuing learning over polished results.
- **Students:** using AI as a partner, not a substitute for thinking.

No one can carry this alone. Parental controls cannot redesign education, teachers cannot supervise every session, and governments cannot legislate judgement into existence. AI companies should not leave the burden on families trying to keep pace.

## The test

AI will keep getting more capable, and children will meet it at home, at school and in every app. Wishing they won't is not a strategy.

A useful test for any educational AI experience is this:

**After using AI, is the child more capable of doing the next task independently, or merely more capable of submitting this one?**
